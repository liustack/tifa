# Optimizations

This study keeps the record of how rendering got faster. The biggest step was the C engine of quickjs v0.29, which renders the pages of the shop, MUI and bench apps in 0.83 to 0.86 of the time of the engine before it. After it, each rewrite the Vite plugin makes to the SSR bundle gains a little. Check the list of ideas that were tried and dropped before you try one again.

[Performance](../performance.md) has the numbers a reader needs to size a machine or compare frameworks. This page has the numbers behind it. "The machine above" and "the machine below" mean the machines described in [How the numbers were measured](../performance.md#how-the-numbers-were-measured). Later releases can change the numbers, so read each one as it was on its day.

## The redesigned shop

The redesign made the shop's pages as heavy as those of a real shop: product photos, a category menu and a cart drawer on every storefront page, a carousel, galleries, reviews with photos and questions, facets and a comparison on the product pages, and an order list of 25 rows a page with filters, row menus and bulk actions. An order shows its log, notes, payment, stock check and packing list, and the stats page a table of every product, a heatmap and the carriers. These numbers are for the new pages. They are not a change in Tifa's speed, so they are not compared with the old ones, except that the order and stats pages are given before and after their second round of modules.

Measured on 2026-10-29 on the machine in [How the numbers were measured](../performance.md#how-the-numbers-were-measured), with Tifa phase5/tifa-switch, quickjs 0cc6d332 (the C engine), built with the profile Tifa shipped then, which made no difference: `BenchmarkShop` in `test/ssr`, one warm VM on one thread of a core whose other thread is idle, 1,000 renders, two runs, the mean of the runs. The last column is the P50 with quickjs v0.28.6, measured on 2026-10-28.

- `BenchmarkShopParallel`, the six pages in turn, on the two threads of one core: one VM renders 31.7 pages a second (25.64 with v0.28.6), and two VMs 39.4 to 39.5 (31.83 with v0.28.6), 1.24 times as many (two runs).

- The storefront pages are heavier still: as HTML, the home page is 342 KB, the product list 469 KB and a product page 341 KB. The storefront has no benchmark of its own yet.

- **Every failure is clean.** No size made the program exit or hang. Each failure was a 500 within the time budget, the VM was replaced, and the next page rendered. `TestExtremeApp` also keeps the program under 1 GiB over all the sizes.
- **Memory runs out first** for large data and long lists: 40 MB of injected data, a table of 5,000 rows, a long Markdown document. **Time runs out first** for long Markdown and for the admin day, which has 300 replies in Markdown: `/markdown/200` and `/console/100` take 0.9 to 1.1 s on the M4 and 2.1 to 2.6 s on the EPYC, close to the 3 seconds of `ssr.timeout`.
- **The Markdown rows are after a fix of micromark.** micromark 4.0.3, the parser under react-markdown, copies every event of the document on each edit it applies, so its time grows with the square of the document's size ([micromark#246](https://github.com/micromark/micromark/issues/246), open). Lists set it off, and highlighting takes only about 2% of the page. The apps of this repository patch it (`patches/micromark-util-edit-map@1.0.0.patch`, the change proposed in that issue), and the rows are with the patch: on the EPYC, 200 KB takes 2.3 s, 1 MB 13 s and 2 MB 25 s to render with 1 GiB and 60 seconds, about 11 to 13 ms per KB. Without the patch the same rows took 2.7 s, 22 s and over 60 s, measured on quickjs main at 0c8c338. An app that renders long Markdown with react-markdown needs the same patch, or micromark 4.0.2, until micromark releases a fix.
- **Long Markdown holds about 0.45 MiB per KB at its peak**, above what a loaded VM holds: 45 MiB at 100 KB, 171 MiB at 400 KB and 447 MiB at 1 MB. The default 128 MiB fits up to about 270 KB. The memory in the cells is read after the render, so a GC during the render can leave it below the peak. Most of this is the libraries: the parser keeps every token of the document alive until it has read all of it, and Node needs a similar amount for the same libraries (its heap peaks at 0.3 to 0.5 MiB per KB, 138 MiB at 400 KB and 281 MiB at 1 MB). The engine's peak is the same as Node's up to 200 KB and 1.3 to 1.6 times it from 400 KB to 1 MB. What the parser keeps alive takes 2.2 to 2.6 times the memory it takes in V8 (an object is 64 bytes with its properties in another block), while Node leaves more garbage waiting for a GC.
- **JSON nests 2,000 levels at most**, so `/json/2001` fails with `RangeError: JSON nesting too deep` after 1 to 2 ms, where the engine before the C engine parsed it.
- **Deep calls cost goroutine stack**, not VM memory. During a render the goroutine stack grows by 1 to 4 MiB for 500 to 1,000 levels of components or providers and for 400 to 600 nested `div`s, and by 8 MiB for 5,000 and 10,000 levels of components, on both machines. Pages that are wide rather than deep grow it by at most 512 KiB.
- **Injected data**: on the M4, the render of the page with 5 MB of data took 40 ms, 32 ms of which went to parsing the data in the VM. Before the render, Go took 21 ms to load the data and write it as JSON, and 2 ms to escape it. Go writes long data texts into the page after the VM is done ([Data protocol](../build.md#data-protocol)), so the VM keeps only the parsed data: 20 MB renders in 160 ms, 131 ms of which is parsing, and 40 MB runs out of memory while parsing.
- **A stopped VM slows others by a few milliseconds**: on the M4, with 4 VMs, the P99 of `/table/25` on three of them was 5 to 6 ms alone, 5 to 6 ms while the fourth rendered `/markdown/200`, and 9 to 10 ms while the fourth was stopped and replaced 7 times in ten seconds (`TestExtremeIsolation`, three runs). The test lets a stop add 5 ms.

## What made it faster

All changes leave the HTML the same byte for byte. Effects are the time a render of the shop's pages takes, measured on the machine above unless the row says otherwise.

### Engine

[docs/changes](https://github.com/liustack/quickjs/blob/main/docs/changes/README.md) of [github.com/liustack/quickjs](https://github.com/liustack/quickjs) lists each change, with the details and tests. The change for speed that matters for the numbers in this doc is the one that replaced the engine before it. The smaller changes of the engine before it, up to v0.28.6, are in git history:

| Change | Release | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The whole engine is QuickJS in C, compiled by LLVM to Go assembly, in place of the Go that ccgo wrote from it | v0.29 | Against quickjs before v0.29.0 (built with the profile Tifa shipped then, the C engine without any), on the AMD EPYC 7B13, one VM on one core, 8 code positions each, 4 rounds in turn: the six workbench pages of the shop in 0.834 to 0.843 of the time, the three pages of the MUI test app in 0.853 to 0.860 and the two of `test/apps/bench` in 0.844 to 0.849, every position faster. The HTML is the same byte for byte over 160 pages of the shop, 33 of the pages app, 60 of MUI and 30 of the extreme app, except the page of 2001 levels of JSON, which now fails. The Go garbage collector stops the program for as long as before while VMs render: a P99 of 131 to 164 µs with 1 VM and 197 µs with 3 |

quickjs v0.29.9 as a whole, against v0.29.8 under the same Tifa and the same SSR bundles, measured on 2026-11-03 on the AMD EPYC 7B13 (linux/amd64): one VM on one core with its hyperthread idle, 2 rounds in turn over 16 code layouts, the sum of the P50 of every page of an app, judged by quickjs's [protocol](https://github.com/liustack/quickjs/blob/main/docs/measuring.md) with a delta of 1%. The release changes the generated engine on amd64 (two out of memory fixes) and adds ports to other platforms. The mean of the layouts: the six workbench pages of the shop -0.20% (-0.54% to +0.14%), the MUI test app's pages -0.25% (-0.60% to +0.11%), the bench app's pages +0.06% (-0.48% to +0.61%) and the storefront pages -0.47% (-0.73% to -0.21%, faster). All four say no regression beyond delta. At the layout that ships, the sums are +0.02%, +0.11%, +0.18% and -0.55%, and one page, the stats page of the other shop, is 0.95% slower there and +0.07% over the layouts. The layouts are every front of the interpreter body at shift 0 and eight shifts that are multiples of 64 (64, 576, 1088, 1664, 2176, 2752, 3264 and 3840), since the odd multiples of 32 of the protocol do not move the interpreter by the amount its check expects on this engine.

quickjs v0.30.1 makes three things stoppable by the timeout that were not: flattening a long rope, `String.prototype.repeat`, and compiling a very long regular expression or a very large character class. Before, compiling a very long pattern under a timeout could kill the VM. Now it is interrupted like any other render. The bytecode a regex compiles to is the same byte for byte. Measured with callgrind: the regex part of the store pages runs about 230,000 fewer instructions per render, the shop, MUI and bench pages are flat, and overall the change is -0.00% to +0.29%, all of it in the GC after the render.

`URL` and `URLSearchParams` of the engine's package `url`, which Tifa registers on every VM in place of the core-js polyfill of the host script. On a site with languages, the router makes a `URL` for each location it builds, since Tifa's prefix rewrite turns on its rewrite path (`new URL(fullPath, origin)` in `buildLocation` of `@tanstack/router-core`), and parses three more per render. Per warm render that is 17 to 40 `URL`s on the six workbench pages of the shop, 74 to 170 on the storefront pages. core-js parsed the base again for each one, made a `URLSearchParams` for each, and serialized the URL on every read of a part. The engine parses in Go and keeps the 11 parts in the object as strings. Measured on the Apple M4 under other load, with an earlier engine than v0.30.3 (the sizes and memory below are from that run too), one process per side and round, the sides in turn, with a copy of the old side as A/A, each page rendered 15 times a round after 20 to warm up: the three storefront pages (`/store`, `/zh-CN/store/products`, a product) in 0.630 of the time over 4 rounds (0.514 to 0.634, A/A 1.009) and 0.692 over 8 (0.550 to 0.710, A/A 1.115), the six workbench pages 0.850 (0.740 to 0.861, A/A 1.042) and 0.869 (0.689 to 1.103, A/A 1.095). The 8 rounds ran on a busier machine and only the storefront stays clear of their A/A. The engine measured 0.63 and 0.85 on its own host on a quiet machine. The HTML is the same byte for byte over 160 pages of the shop and the three of the MUI test app. A site without languages builds no `URL` in a render. With core-js gone from the host script, the script is 47 KB smaller, the bytecode of the shop 199 KB smaller (4.43 MB), a VM holds 119 KiB less after loading (8,168 KiB) and 213 KiB less after rendering every page, and loading a VM takes as long as before (about 30 ms).

Each item says what it would give and why it was dropped. Check here before trying one of them again.

### Engine

These were tried on the engine that translated C to Go, which quickjs v0.29 replaced. The ideas still apply to the C engine, so they stay. The numbers are from the old engine. What only made sense for the Go translation (TLS frames, libc's lock, the Go stack frame of the interpreter) is gone.

- **Literal templates in the bytecode**, as V8 copies a prebuilt object: needs compiler and bytecode format changes, and quickjs-ng saw no clear difference when it tried.
- **Allocating the property array of a literal at its final size**: the opcode that makes the object does not know the count, and growing arrays differently costs memory for every small object. It pays only together with kept shapes, which v0.26.16 has. Not tried on top of them.
- **Inline caches for property reads**: quickjs-ng added and then removed them. Across 17 real tools they ranged from 11% slower to 54% faster, 4% faster on average, and used more memory. Not tried.
- **Writing the bit fields of a new object in 2 stores rather than 9**: no change (0.991 to 1.010). The time is the cache miss of the first write to the new object, and fewer stores only move it.
- **A fast path for object spread** (`JS_CopyDataProperties`): one spread took 240 ns rather than 415 in a micro benchmark on the Apple M4, but the shop did not change (0.997 to 1.005).
- **Dropping function source from the bytecode** (`JS_SetStripInfo`) to save VM memory: seroval reads the source of its own helpers with `toString` and writes it into pages, so the browser would break. The VMs of a pool share one copy of that source instead, see [Memory](../performance.md#memory).
- **Go versions of `TextEncoder`, `AbortController` and the other host polyfills** (`URL` is the engine's now, see [Engine](#engine)): counted on the 17 pages of the shop and the 3 of the MUI test app, a warm render makes 7 to 9 `AbortController`s, adds one listener to a signal and queues at most one microtask, about 11 to 15 µs, 0.02% to 0.24% of the render. It calls `TextEncoder`, `TextDecoder`, `Headers`, `Response`, `structuredClone`, `atob` and `btoa` not at all, so render time has nothing to gain. They are slow when called: on the Apple M4, `TextEncoder.encode` of 4,000 characters takes about 2.9 ms and `structuredClone` of a 200-byte object about 180 µs, 37 times a JSON round trip. Revisit for an app that calls them while rendering.

Weighed for the engine and never built:

- **A JIT written in pure Go**, turning the bytecode of hot functions into machine code. Counted on the shop's pages, machine code would call back into the engine 39,000 to 137,000 times a render (about 100 times per 1,000 opcodes). Each call out costs 3.7 to 14 ns more than today's Go call on an AMD EPYC (1.5 to 2 ns on an Apple M4), which takes back 1.6% to 5.8% of a render. The net estimate is 1.11 to 1.30 times as fast, about 1.2, for 3 to 6 months of work, and only if the machine code removes 40% to 60% of the interpreter's own time, which nobody measured. In the comparison of pure Go engines on 2026-10-21 the JIT mode was slower than the interpreter of the same engine for go-quickjs (19.0 ms against 14.9 ms for a React render) and faster for jsc2go (14.1 against 20.0) and goant (28.0 against 34.1). Not started. Revisit only with an approach unlike those of jsc2go, goant and go-quickjs.
- **Fused bytecodes** (superinstructions): 40 fused pairs and triples would cut 19% of the dispatches (31% on MUI pages, 5% on the workbench pages), modeled at 1.3% to 2.2% fewer instructions. The one real fused pair built measured no faster than its control, but it was too small to count instructions, so that test proves nothing. Shelved until the arithmetic fast paths are done, then redo it counting instructions.
- **Leaving built-in objects out of a VM**: all the built-ins of a bare context take about 67 KB and 0.14 ms (measured on quickjs v0.26.16), and the largest group, typed arrays, 12.6 KB and 34 µs, so there is no memory or start-up time to win. Cutting Atomics `wait` and `notify`, `SharedArrayBuffer`, `HTMLDDA` or modules would save a few KB to 100 KB each, fail test262 and cost work on every sync with upstream. Pages use more of the language than it seems: `Proxy` (i18next, immer, zod, TanStack Query), `BigInt` and `new Function` (zod), typed arrays, generators, async functions and `\p{}` regular expressions.

### Build

- **A JavaScript cache and a `replaceAll` chain for React's escape**: they were the escape of a VM without the engine's `__tifa_escape`, and every VM of the pool has it, so no render ran them. With them turned off, a render runs -0.05% to -0.09% instructions on the shop's workbench pages, -0.02% to -0.03% on its storefront pages, +0.02% to -0.14% on the MUI pages and +0.02% to +0.03% on the bench pages (callgrind, one warm render of every page of an app, quickjs v0.29.8, linux/amd64), inside the 0.11% by which two runs of one bundle differ. A VM without the engine function runs React's own escape now.
- **Working out the key of an element in `retryNode` only when the key path or a replay reads it**: with the key paths gone, only a task with a replay reads it, and no task of `renderToString` has one. With the rewrite turned off, a render runs +0.05% instructions on the shop's workbench pages, +0.03% on its storefront pages, -0.04% on the MUI pages and +0.11% on the bench pages (callgrind, one warm render of every page of an app, quickjs v0.29.8, linux/amd64), inside the 0.11% by which two runs of one bundle differ. Three tests of a key a render does not read cost less than the interpreter's own noise.
- **Hoisting static JSX out of components**, as Babel's transform-react-constant-elements does: the shop's pages build 44 to 83 fewer elements but render only 0.5% to 0.9% faster, and an almost static page 5.4%. One element saved is about 0.8 µs: the time goes to React walking the tree and joining strings. It cannot reach the JSX of libraries in `node_modules`, needs Babel or a new transform, and a hoisted element is shared by all requests of a VM, so a library that changes one would carry that change into the next request.
- **Folding calls of `cn`, `clsx` and `cva` with literal arguments into strings at build time**: only 1 of the 143 calls of `cn` in the SSR bundle of the example shop has nothing but literals (`cn("min-h-[50vh] border")`), and none of the 99 calls of `clsx` in the MUI test app and none in the bench app. The rest pass a prop (`className`), a condition or a member (`classes.root`). The six `cva` calls of the shop have only literals, but each runs once when its module loads, and the function it returns is called with props from variables (`buttonVariants({ variant, size })`). A render has no call to fold.
- **Caching the result of `clsx` and `cn` by their string arguments**: `cn` 0.5 already caches by the arguments of its last calls and by the string it merges. A prototype with one more cache in front of it ran 0.47% more instructions on the shop's workbench pages and 0.19% more on its storefront pages. For `clsx` (99.9% of the calls of the MUI test app have only strings and falsy values), a wrapper that builds a key from the arguments and asks a `Map` took 390 to 650 ns a call in a micro benchmark with the argument shapes of the MUI pages, where `clsx` takes 315 to 400 ns. Building the key from the strings costs about what `clsx` costs to join them. Writing the join without the loop gains instead, see [Build](#build).
- **A lower syntax target for the SSR bundle**: ES2021 to ES2018 stay within 1%. From ES2017, object spread becomes helper functions, and the shop gets 33% to 45% slower. The target stays ES2022.
- **An unminified SSR bundle**: the bundle is already minified. Unminified, on the shop before the storefront was added, it renders about 1% slower and is 1,799 KB instead of 772, and a VM holds 9,133 KiB after loading instead of 7,634.
- **A test in JavaScript in front of a spread in `createElement` and `cloneElement`**: the plugin tested the config (its prototype, symbols, `__proto__`, `__self` and `__source`, a getter behind `key`) and then copied it with the rest of a destructuring. The test took 310 to 380 ns a call, more than React's loop takes for a config of none, one or two keys, so those elements took up to 1.57 times as long, and the pages took 0.99 of the time at best (1.6% to 2.5% fewer instructions). It also left a proxy, a getter that deletes a later key and a setter added to `Object.prototype` after React loaded as differences. The engine function does the same checks in C, rules those out and gives 2.1% to 4.1% fewer instructions.
- **The tree context of React's string renderer as a number until it overflows**: React makes an object `{id, overflow}` for each child of each array to give `useId` its place. A number does for the context while `overflow` is empty, and the ids it gives are the same, also past the 30 bits where they overflow (compared with React's own on a chain of arrays 70 levels deep and on arrays of 1,500 children). It is not faster on the whole: a push from a number saves 11 ns and one from an object takes 18 ns more for the extra test (3,000,000 calls on an Apple M4), so a page gains only where its ids have not overflowed. Against the build without it the shop's workbench pages run 0.07% more instructions, the storefront pages 0.09% more, the MUI pages 0.28% more and the bench pages 0.29% fewer.
- **The handling of a thrown value in functions of their own, outside `renderNode`**: `renderNode` has a `try` and `catch` around every node, and the catch is most of its text. Moved out (the two calls of `renderNodeDestructive` stay on their lines), the shop's pages run 0.01% fewer instructions, the storefront pages 0.04% more, the MUI pages 0.02% fewer and the bench pages 0.00%: the frame keeps the same 10 locals, since the saved values still have to be passed on.
- **The type of an attribute value in a variable**, `"string" === (t = typeof value) || "function" !== t && ...`, or in a fourth parameter of `pushAttribute`: the variable took 0.02% to 0.06% more instructions than asking `typeof value` again, and the parameter 0.1% to 0.3% more, since each call of `pushAttribute` pays for the one more slot whether the attribute needs it or not.
- **Changes to `flushSegment`, `flushSubtree` and the writes of chunks**: in a round of the shop's six workbench pages `flushSegment` runs 18 times and `flushSubtree` 12 times (15 and 10 for the five storefront pages, 9 and 6 for the three MUI pages), under three times a page, since the pages have few Suspense boundaries. Before the attribute change above those six pages pushed 74,855 chunks, and each segment hands its chunks over in one `join`, which is one call of the engine. With the HTML left out of the page (a bundle that writes an empty string where it joins), a round runs 4.6% fewer instructions, so the joining and the handling of the page string afterwards cannot come to more. A rewrite in JavaScript has nothing to take from it. What is left is fewer chunks, which the attribute change gave (an attribute is 3 in place of 5).
- **Smaller rewrites of React**, each under 1%: `push(x)` as an index assignment (0.1% to 0.9%), spread instead of `for...in` in `jsxProd` (also copies symbol keys, so not the same; `createElement` and `cloneElement` ask the engine instead, see [Build](#build)), an object instead of a `Map` in `startChunkForTag` and `endChunkForTag` (under 0.5%), `Object.keys` instead of `for...in` (slower in a micro benchmark), `Object.hasOwn` instead of `hasOwnProperty.call` (saves about 25 ns per key).
- **Joining the chunks of an attribute with `+`** in `pushAttribute` and `pushStringAttribute` (`" " + name + '="' + value + '"'` pushed as one chunk): on top of the calls written out it saved 0.3% of the instructions of the shop's workbench pages, where pushing the chunks one by one saves 0.8%. The join copies short strings, which costs about what the pushes it saves do. It is the idea of React PR 37022 ([study](react-layer.md)), which made renders slower too.
- **`=== true` in place of `hasOwnProperty.call` on the two caches of `isAttributeNameSafe`**: On the EPYC core of the tests it saves 9 ns a call (58.7 ns against 49.6, over 2,000,000 calls with 22 names the cache holds). With the fast path of `hasOwnProperty.call` in quickjs v0.29.1 the two forms take almost the same time, so the page is not rewritten.
- **A cache in front of the C escape**, the way the JavaScript escape had one: the JavaScript function with its cache takes 340 ns for a class name of 30 characters the cache holds, and the C function escapes the same name in 70 ns, so the pool's VMs go without. A text of 512 characters with 171 to escape that the cache holds is the one case where the cache is faster (899 ns against 2,061 ns), and pages hardly have such a text.
- **Other forms of React's escape and of its search**: `indexOf` five times and a loop over characters are both slower than its regular expression, and five `indexOf` calls and `search` are slower than `exec`. Looking up every string in the escape cache (since removed) before the regular expression made pages of plain text 4% slower, and the 1,188 escapes of an order list 22% slower in a benchmark of those escapes alone, since most strings need no escape and a `Map` lookup costs more than the search. So the cache only takes strings that need escaping. What is left of the search is the engine's. A cache that stops taking strings when full would let the text of early requests keep the room for good, and one that drops the least recently used string pays a delete and a set on each hit. Escaping in Go, through a builtin the escape calls before the regular expression, made the shop 1.3% to 2.0% slower with the same HTML: each call copies the string out of the engine and the result back in, which costs more than the search and the cache. Escaping in C inside the engine does not pay that copy: quickjs ships the function (`RegisterEscapeText`) and the plugin uses it (see Build above).
- **Building React's component stacks and key path names in a cheaper form** instead of dropping them: inlining `pushComponentStack` into its caller, or getting the name of a string or function type without calling `getComponentNameFromType`, stays within 1% either way (0.994 to 1.024 on the shop). The cost is making the objects and strings, not the calls.
- **Rewriting react-spring and TanStack Table in the plugin**: react-spring's color interpolation tests each value against a regular expression of 148 color names, 24 µs a call in QuickJS against 0.175 µs in Node with `--jitless` (2.1% of the order list), and TanStack Table calls through `fn(this, ...args)`, about 1 µs against 67 ns (1.2%). A rewrite would tie the plugin to how two libraries are written. Both are for the engine: test the first character of each alternative of a regular expression before trying it, and spread a plain array without the iterator.
- **Caching parts of a page that do not change between requests as HTML**: Tifa cannot know how a page is written. the render cache keeps whole pages by their data instead.
- **React Compiler**: the `reactCompilerPreset()` of `@vitejs/plugin-react` compiles only the browser build, so the SSR bundle is byte for byte the same and the shop's 20 pages render within 1% either way (0.994 to 1.010), with the same throughput on two VMs. The browser JS grows 6.1% gzipped (31 KB), and the HTML and hydration stay the same. Tifa leaves it off. An app that wants it for the browser can turn it on in its `vite.config.ts` and measure its own interactions and bundle size.
- **Writing static start tags at build time**: the SSR plugin wrote ahead, as strings that React DOM itself made during the build, the start tags of JSX elements whose attributes are all literals. The shop's 6 workbench pages, the storefront's 12 and the 5 MUI pages rendered in the same time, each round's ratio on both sides of 1. Only two pages of `test/apps/bench` built for it gained (the list 8.1%, the spiral 7.3%): on real pages these tags are a small part of a render, next to components, hooks and styles. It made the shop's SSR bundle 4.9% larger and each VM about 200 KB larger, and it checked React DOM's internals by hash, so every React release would need an update.
- **Writing page data with seroval's `crossSerialize` when nothing in it waits**, instead of the stream writer TanStack Router uses: the text is the same, but no page got faster (the shop's workbench took 1.004 of the time, the storefront 1.002, a table of 1,000 rows 0.999), and it needed a list of known plugins and a scan of the data first.
- **Off-the-shelf inlining before the minifier**: oxc (the minifier of rolldown, and `oxc-minify` 0.156.0) and esbuild 0.28.2 do not write out the calls of function declarations of this size. terser 5.51.2 (`inline: 3`) writes out only functions that return a constant and functions called once, and the calls that run fell by 0.02% to 0.6%. swc 1.16.21 (`compress.inline: 3`) cut them by 0.2% to 1.3%, but its other rewrites made the shop's workbench pages and the MUI pages run 2.8% to 3.2% more instructions. So the plugin writes the calls out itself.
- **A temporary variable for an argument that breaks the order rule** of [writing out small functions](../build.md#frontend-output): counted on the shop's workbench pages, it would let 1.5 points more of the calls that run be written out (6.0% in place of 4.6%), about 0.3% of the instructions before the cost of the variables. Not built.

### Server

- **A full collection every 64 or 1024 renders** next to the young ones, against every 256: every 64 raised the P99 of the shop by 1.5% to 4%, and every 1024 gave the same P99 and memory as 256, with 2 full collections in a round against 8.
- **A young collection only every 2, 4 or 8 renders, or only after 1 MiB more was allocated**, against one after each render: the CPU a render took stayed within the spread of the rounds on the shop, the storefront and the MUI pages, and the engine ran 0.4% more instructions on the shop at every 4, while the heap of a VM at its peak grew by 7% to 50%. A young collection costs about the same for each object it looks at (130 to 145 ns, mostly cache misses), so collecting half as often looks at twice the objects. Not emptying the cache of small free blocks after each collection saved nothing either: the frees that follow fill it sooner and take the slow path. The young collection after each render is 2.0% of a shop request's CPU and 1.1% of a MUI request's.
- **Other parts of a request.** One VM on one core, gzip, the shop's pages: the render is 94% of a request's CPU (97% for MUI), the young collection 2.0%, gzip 2.2%, the handler, `Load`, Go's GC, the data slots and the JSON of the render input 1.0% together. gzip at level 1 would save 0.7% for pages 21% larger, and `GOGC=400` at most 0.2% for about 25 MB more. None reaches 1% of throughput, so nothing outside the render was changed.
- **A higher collection threshold**, twice or three times the live size or a fixed 32 MiB, with the engine still collecting during renders: a collection takes about as long as the garbage it frees, so collecting half as often saved only about 15% of the collection time, under 1% of a render, too little to measure. The P99 and P999 got worse, a VM held 40% to 50% more, and with a low `ssr.vm_memory` a VM ran out of memory sooner.
- **Collecting between renders only at twice the live size**, with three times during a render: the same P99 as at 1.5 times, but a VM held 22% to 45% more, and with a low `ssr.vm_memory` it ran out of memory sooner.
- **Skipping the search for a Suspense boundary left to the browser when `onError` got no error**: the runtime looks for `<!--$!-->` in the whole HTML after each render, about 1% of a shop render and 0.4% of a MUI page, and skipping it renders the shop in 0.987 of the time and the MUI pages in 0.995. But React 19.3 leaves a boundary to the browser without calling `onError` when a component calls `use(browser())`: `renderToString` reports that to an `onBrowserBailout` option it does not take. So the search stays, and making it cheaper is the engine's job.
- **zstd at its fastest level, and brotli**: zstd at gzhttp's fastest level takes 82 to 138 µs a page, but the pages come out 3% to 11% larger than with gzip. Brotli at levels 4 and 5, with the pure Go encoder github.com/andybalholm/brotli, takes 4 to 6 times as long as gzip for pages 3% to 16% smaller. For the files the build compresses, zstd and brotli at their best levels give files 5% and 14% smaller than gzip at its best. Tifa compresses pages with gzip at level 5 and zstd at its default level, as Caddy and Traefik do, gzip when the client takes both, and the build writes gzip files.
- **Rendering with React's stream renderers so that pages wait for lazy components**: `prerender` from `react-dom/static` and `renderToReadableStream` waited on `allReady` give the same HTML, but rendered the shop 8.7 to 8.8 times slower and the MUI pages 4.2 times slower, with up to 1.7 MiB more in each VM, on Tifa 6e062d6. Rendering with `renderToString` first and again with a stream renderer only when it left a boundary to the browser would keep ordinary pages as fast, but would render those pages twice. Tifa renders with `prerenderToString` instead, a string render that the plugin adds to React DOM and that waits for what the jobs of the VM settle: over 3 layouts of 6 rounds on the GCP machine,, the shop rendered in 0.995 of the time of `renderToString` and the MUI pages in 1.001, with the same HTML and each VM about 20 to 40 KiB larger.
- **A built-in TanStack Query**: Tifa gives pages the router's own cache and nothing more, and an app that wants Query can still use it in the browser. On an order page of an early prototype, the official `@tanstack/react-router-ssr-query` made the render 12.1% to 12.9% slower (P50, 3 rounds). It also needed a `ReadableStream` polyfill in each VM, left two global variables in the browser and made the SSR bundle 27% larger. Query's own `dehydrate()` written by hand cost 8.1% to 8.3%, and passing only `[key, data, updatedAt]` cost 4.0% to 4.3%. One loader per route cost nothing measurable (within 0.3%).
- **A form of a `Load`'s data for the VM that is faster than JSON text**: the VM reads the data with `JSON.parse`, which is C in the engine. In callgrind it takes 61 instructions a byte on the table of 1,000 rows (5.6M of the 131M instructions of a render, 92 KB of data) and 136 a byte on the order list of the example shop (1.4M of 277M, 10 KB), which is 0.5% to 4.3% of a render. Making the JavaScript string of the text out of Go's string takes 2.7 instructions a byte (0.25M for the table), so that copy is all a different hand-over could save outside the engine: about 4% of the parse and at most 0.2% of a render. Parsing the text straight from Go's buffer needs a call quickjs does not have, and any other form has to build the same objects in the VM. A faster parse is the engine's to find (`JSON.parse` could remember the atoms and shapes of the keys it just read, and the rows of a table repeat the same keys), and was not measured here.
- **Cutting the fixed cost of the router's own hydration data**: the matches of the router (their ids, status, times and loader data) and the build ID go into the page through seroval's `crossSerializeStream`, which takes the same work for every page whatever its data. Measured on the six workbench pages of the shop with callgrind (instructions a page, about 4.8 instructions a nanosecond on the machine above): 1.9M (1.3% of a render, about 0.4 ms) for that call, 0.1M for the matches, plugins and build ID around it, and 0.07M for the hydration script tags. The MUI test app's pages take 0.7M, and the bench page with no data 1.8M. Of the 1.9M, 0.9M (0.19 ms) is the three plugins TanStack Router adds to every page, for errors and for two kinds of stream, which each ask about every value of the matches: a page that leaves them out runs 0.61% fewer instructions. A `Load` of Tifa returns JSON and no stream, but a plugin is how an `Error` in a match reaches the page, and the list is TanStack's, so Tifa would have to rewrite a function of TanStack Router, in the files of every release, to leave them out. Writing the `$R` references that TanStack's client reads back ourselves would break with the next release. Not done.

## Where to continue

The time of one render is nearly all the cost: HTTP, compression and the rest take under 1 ms of the 13.7 ms of CPU a workbench request takes. Anything that makes a render faster raises throughput by as much. In rough order of what it may give:

1. **The C engine against C QuickJS.** With the engine before it, quickjs took 1.17 to 1.26 times as long as C on the same JavaScript. The C engine runs the C itself, compiled to Go assembly, so what is left is the cost of that compilation and of the calls into Go. Measure it with quickjs's engine comparison, and see where the rest goes, before choosing what to change.
2. **Regular expressions.** The journal posts render in 1.24 times the time of C, while the other pages of the shop are faster than C: most of their time is the syntax highlighter's regular expressions.
3. **Making and freeing objects.** React makes an object for every element, hook and component on the stack. The plugin already keeps React from building component stacks no one reads. The rest only the engine can fix.
4. **Kept shapes with less memory.** Keeping the shapes of object literals saves render time but costs memory in each VM. A change in the C, such as a reference from each child shape to its parent, could give the same speed with less memory. A candidate for upstream.
5. **New Go versions.** The engine is assembly that depends on how Go lays out stacks and registers. When a Go version ships, run the checks listed in quickjs's `docs/upgrading.md`, and measure again.
6. **Whole-page cache hits.** Measure a hit of `Cache` against a hit of Next.js ISR, the same idea of a page kept for everyone for a time, on pages of a test app that has `Cache` (`test/apps/pagecache`), or on a route added for the experiment, since the shop no longer uses `Cache`. A hit of the render cache (the 80 times in [CPU and `ssr.vms`](../performance.md#cpu-and-ssrvms)) still runs the `Load` functions, a hit of `Cache` does not, so the two Tifa numbers must not be mixed up. Compare on one core with and without compression.
7. **Comparisons not made yet.** Next.js and TanStack Start on two or more cores, with compression and for start-up on the redesigned pages, on more than two cores, and with the storefront's pages, and the whole shop on a 1-core VPS with 1 GB of memory.
