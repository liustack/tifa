Every product photo in the shop is taken in our workshop, with no studio and no assistant. Many people ask how the kettle shot was made, so here it is.

## The kit

Our kit is plain:

- A full-frame camera we have used for six years, with a 90 mm macro lens
- One 300 W continuous light in a 60 cm octagonal softbox
- Two foam white cards and one black card
- A roll of white PVC backdrop from the hardware store

| Kit | Cost | Can you skip it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Camera and lens | About $1,300 used | A new phone does well too |
| Continuous light | About $170 | Soft light by a window will do |
| White and black cards | About $6 | No, the best money we spent |

## The light

A steel kettle is a mirror that shows everything around it. So the point of the light is not to light it, but to choose what it reflects.

1. The softbox goes behind the kettle on the left, a little higher than it, for one long highlight.
2. A white card on the right sends light back, so the shadows do not go black.
3. A small black card under the spout gives its edge a dark outline, which makes it look crisp.

> When you shoot something shiny, decide first what it will reflect, then where the light comes from.

## The shot

The aperture goes to f/8 to f/11, so everything from spout to handle is sharp. Shutter speed does not matter: the camera is on a tripod and a continuous light does not flash. We take a series at different focus points and merge them afterwards, which is called focus stacking.

## The edit

The edit does three things: it removes dust, sets the white balance and makes the backdrop pure white. It does not change the kettle's shape or add a sheen that is not there. What customers get should look like the picture.

At the end every image is exported as WebP at 2000 pixels wide, and the web shop scales it to the screen.
